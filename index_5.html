Section 5What the published data supports about program design

Program counts describe what the program did. Published data establishes what it is aimed at, and whether the aim is right. Everything in this section is real, from the 2026 Alaska Native Injury Atlas — unlike the program figures above, nothing here is illustrative.

How we report regional data, and why. The atlas publishes figures for thirteen regions. We report the Interior’s own figures alongside the statewide picture, and we do not rank regions against one another. A comparative table invites reading one community’s experience as a score against another’s, which serves no purpose for this program and is not ours to publish. Where comparison aids interpretation, we give the statewide range.

What this figure is, and the mistake it invites. The denominator is total injury hospitalizations in the region, not regional population. So this is a share of injury burden, not a rate, and a region with fewer injuries of other kinds shows a higher percentage without any difference in suicide attempts at all. The atlas is explicit that it publishes no rates based on population at any geography.

A higher share can also reflect better identification and coding rather than more events — which, in a prevention program, is a sign of a system working.

Age and sex distribution, statewide

The grant serves youth and young adults up to age 24. This distribution is the evidence that it is the right population to serve.

Age groupFemaleMaleTotalShare of all

The program is aimed where prevention reaches furthest. Ages 10 to 29 account for 62.7% of these hospitalizations statewide. The grant’s up-to-24 population sits inside the age range where gatekeeper training and screening put the program in contact with the most young people, so the funded design is already pointed at the right place. The evaluation’s job is to show how far it reaches.

Where screening effort goes furthest per hour. Young women are 78.0% of the 10–19 figures. Placing screening where young women already are — school health, routine clinic visits, youth programming — reaches the most people for a given amount of staff time. That is a reach decision, not a risk judgment about any individual, and it is why screening reach is reported by sex each quarter.

Every person in this table reached care. These are hospitalizations, so each one represents someone identified, admitted and treated. The system engaged. What a prevention program adds is earlier contact — a trained adult, a screening conversation, a referral that connects — which is precisely what the five indicators in Section 1 measure.
